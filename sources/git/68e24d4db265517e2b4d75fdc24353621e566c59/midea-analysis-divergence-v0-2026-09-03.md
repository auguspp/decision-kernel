## 2. Frame admissibility gate

The goal is not to manufacture four stories.

A frame is admissible only when the same PIT record supports it as a reasonable economic model class or underwriting lens.

### Frame A — mature cash-return franchise

**Admissible: YES.**

Economic model class:

> **A mature, high-quality appliance / consumer-industrial franchise whose Smart Home cash engine grows slowly, returns a large share of earnings to owners, and should be valued primarily on durable core cash generation rather than heroic reinvestment.**

Why reasonable:

- Smart Home remains the dominant business and mature cash engine;
- 2026H1 growth slowed materially from 2025 but stayed positive;
- realized dividends are large;
- 2025 operating cash generation was strong;
- long historical ROE and mature domestic positioning support franchise durability;
- newer growth pools are heterogeneous rather than uniformly excellent.

This frame does not assume stagnation. It simply gives the burden of proof to incremental growth outside the mature core.

### Frame B — global own-brand / OBM compounder

**Admissible: YES, WITH EVIDENCE LIMITATION.**

Economic model class:

> **A global consumer brand and supply-chain platform that can use overseas own-brand expansion to extend growth duration while preserving attractive margins and capital efficiency.**

Why reasonable:

- overseas revenue is already economically material and grew faster than domestic revenue in H1;
- the group has established global manufacturing, channel and product capabilities;
- Smart Home can finance continued brand expansion;
- overseas mix is a plausible source of longer growth duration than mature domestic appliances alone.

Evidence limitation:

> Current frozen evidence is much stronger on overseas revenue than on clean overseas OBM operating margin, working capital and incremental ROIC.

Therefore this is an admissible growth model, but not a proven high-ROIC rebase.

### Frame C — B2B reinvestment platform

**Admissible: YES, WITH HETEROGENEITY WARNING.**

Economic model class:

> **A capital allocator using a mature consumer cash engine to build higher-duration B2B growth pools in building technology, industrial technology, robotics and adjacent industrial businesses.**

Why reasonable:

- Building Technologies and Robotics both showed real H1 growth;
- Building Technologies currently has attractive reported gross-margin economics;
- Midea has enough cash generation to fund adjacent growth without relying solely on external capital;
- a multi-business industrial platform can extend duration if incremental returns remain high.

Heterogeneity warning:

- Industrial Technology declined materially in H1 and had lower gross-margin economics;
- B2B revenue growth is not equivalent to high incremental ROIC;
- the aggregate `Commercial & Industrial Solutions` label can hide very different economic species.

This frame is admissible only if the analysis remains segment-specific.

### Frame D — capital-allocation / per-share owner-return lens

**Admissible: YES, BUT NOT AN INDEPENDENT MODEL CLASS.**

Underwriting lens:

> **Whatever growth narrative is used, value is determined by the same owner-return translation: per-share owner-earnings growth + cash dividends + true net share shrinkage +/- valuation change.**

This lens is strongly supported by the realized dividend, share-cancellation, employee-plan repurchase and cash-generation evidence.

But it is not a fourth independent vote.

It is a common translation layer applied to Frames A-C.

### Admissibility verdict

```text
A = admissible mature-franchise model class
B = admissible global-growth model class, evidence-limited
C = admissible B2B-reinvestment model class, heterogeneous
D = admissible owner-return lens, NOT independent
```

Already one important possibility appears:

> Frames B and C may also be less independent than their strategic labels suggest, because both ultimately require attractive incremental ROIC on retained capital outside the mature Smart Home core.

The experiment must test that rather than count them as separate experts.

## 3. Pre-expectation underwriting by frame

Published sell-side estimates are withheld in this section.

### 3.1 Frame A — mature cash-return franchise

Pre-expectation view:

```text
Smart Home remains resilient and cash generative
+ domestic growth is mature
+ payout is already high
+ newer segments are mixed
-> core durability is strong
-> future EPS growth should be moderate unless reinvestment pools prove unusually productive
-> a large portion of owner return should come from dividend + disciplined per-share accretion
```

Natural owner-return prior:

- low-to-mid single-digit per-share earnings growth is easier to support than high-single-digit growth;
- payout can remain high if reinvestment needs are modest;
- terminal valuation should reflect quality and durability, but not require a new growth model class.

Main falsifier:

> Smart Home economics structurally deteriorate, or core cash is increasingly used to subsidize low-return adjacencies.

### 3.2 Frame B — global OBM compounder

Pre-expectation view:

```text
mature domestic core
+ meaningful overseas revenue base
+ global supply chain / channel capability
+ higher overseas growth
-> overseas own-brand mix can extend duration
but
-> revenue growth must translate into margin, working-capital efficiency and incremental ROIC
```

Natural owner-return prior:

- mid-single-digit to potentially higher per-share growth is plausible;
- the key is not overseas revenue growth alone but overseas retained-capital productivity;
- a higher terminal multiple is justified only if global growth proves durable and capital-light enough.

Main falsifier:

> overseas growth requires persistent low-return marketing, manufacturing or channel capital, leaving group per-share growth little better than the mature-core case.

### 3.3 Frame C — B2B reinvestment platform

Pre-expectation view:

```text
mature Smart Home cash engine
+ Building Tech growth / margin
+ Robotics growth
+ industrial capability
-> selected B2B pools can absorb retained capital
but
-> Industrial Tech weakness and segment heterogeneity make aggregate B2B growth an unsafe shortcut
```

Natural owner-return prior:

- higher growth requires more capital retention unless asset turns / capital intensity improve materially;
- payout and growth may therefore trade off more directly than in Frame A;
- the decisive variable is segment-level incremental ROIC, not consolidated B2B revenue growth.

Main falsifier:

> retained capital rises but B2B returns remain mediocre, turning the mature core into a funding source for conglomerate dilution.

### 3.4 Frame D — capital-allocation / per-share lens

Pre-expectation view:

Frame D refuses to choose a strategic growth story first.

It asks:

```text
per-share owner return
≈ per-share owner-earnings growth
+ cash dividend yield
+ genuine net share shrinkage
+/- valuation change
```

and:

```text
growth
≈ retention ratio
x incremental return on retained capital
```

This immediately creates a common denominator for Frames A-C.

Illustrative arithmetic only:

- with ~30% retention, ~3% sustainable growth requires roughly ~10% incremental return on retained capital;
- ~6% growth requires roughly ~20%;
- ~8% growth requires roughly ~27%.

These are not forecasts or measured Midea segment ROICs. They show why strategic narratives must eventually translate into capital productivity.

## 4. Pre-expectation divergence test

Before revealing sell-side estimates, compare the frames on causal structure.

### 4.1 What genuinely differs

Frame A places the default burden on maturity:

> owner return comes mostly from resilient core cash + payout + modest growth.

Frames B/C place more burden on reinvestment:

> owner return requires retained capital to create a longer growth runway.

That is a meaningful economic distinction.

### 4.2 What begins to collapse

However, Frames B and C do not produce fundamentally different owner-return equations.

Both reduce to:

```text
new growth pool
-> revenue / margin
-> working capital + capex / marketing investment
-> incremental ROIC
-> sustainable per-share growth
```

The label on the growth pool differs:

- overseas OBM;
- Building Tech / Robotics / Industrial Tech.

But the causal burden is substantially the same.

### 4.3 Frame D exposes the common model

Frame D shows that even Frame A is not a separate mathematical species.

All three strategic frames can be translated into:

```text
core durability
+ retention
x incremental ROIC
+ cash payout
+ true net share shrinkage
+/- valuation change
```

This is the first major difference from GigaDevice.

For GigaDevice, the frame battle changed whether high earnings were scarcity rent or durable platform economics and therefore changed the terminal model class itself.

For Midea, the strategic frames mostly change **parameter expectations inside one owner-return model**.

That distinction will determine whether Analysis Divergence is probability-relevant.

## 9. Frame-sensitive conclusions

Material frame sensitivity remains, but it is mainly continuous rather than model-discrete.

### 9.1 Sustainable growth rate

Frame A naturally supports lower growth.

Frames B/C can support higher growth if overseas / B2B incremental returns are high.

### 9.2 Retention versus payout

Frame A can rationally sustain a high payout because mature reinvestment needs are lower.

A genuine B/C rebase may justify retaining more capital — but only if that capital earns attractive incremental returns.

Therefore a lower payout would not automatically strengthen the thesis.

### 9.3 Terminal multiple

Frame A supports a quality-franchise multiple.

Frames B/C may justify a higher multiple if growth duration and capital efficiency are proven.

But exact terminal P/E is market-dependent and should not be laundered into Fundamental probability.

### 9.4 True net share shrinkage

A mature-franchise frame may rely more heavily on cancellation / per-share accretion.

A reinvestment frame may need more capital for growth and therefore less aggressive cancellation.

Again, the difference is a capital-allocation parameter inside one owner-return model.

## 10. Load-bearing disagreements

Four unresolved variables matter for owner value.

### 10.1 Smart Home durability

Question:

> Can the mature cash engine preserve margin, share and cash conversion while funding global / B2B growth?

This is strongly evidenced relative to other variables, but not immutable.

### 10.2 Overseas OBM incremental ROIC

Question:

> Does overseas own-brand growth translate into attractive margin, working-capital efficiency and capital returns?

Current evidence is insufficiently granular to establish this.

### 10.3 B2B incremental ROIC

Question:

> Which B2B pools can absorb retained capital at returns high enough to preserve group per-share compounding?

The H1 segment record is heterogeneous and therefore unresolved.

### 10.4 Net capital-allocation accretion

Question:

> After dividends, cancellation, employee incentives, dilution and financing, how much genuine per-share accretion reaches owners?

This requires prospective share-count and capital-allocation evidence.

### Key distinction from GigaDevice

These disagreements affect valuation, but they mostly enter the same equation:

```text
retention
x incremental ROIC
-> per-share earnings growth

+ cash dividend
+ true net share shrinkage
+/- valuation multiple
-> owner return
```

They do **not** currently require mutually incompatible terminal economic model classes.

## 13. Failure-condition audit

The experiment was designed to fail if apparently different frames became narrative re-labelings of the same economics.

That failure condition is **substantially met**.

### What did not survive as independent divergence

```text
global OBM compounder
vs
B2B reinvestment platform
```

look strategically different, but both require:

```text
retained capital
-> attractive incremental ROIC
-> sustainable per-share growth
```

Frame D then translates both into the same owner-return equation.

### What remains genuinely different

The useful remaining contrast is narrower:

```text
mature cash-return baseline
vs
reinvestment-driven growth upside
```

Even that is better represented as a **continuous capital-allocation trade-off** than as mutually exclusive world classes.

### Negative-control conclusion

> **Analysis Divergence is not a universal Research primitive. It is most useful when admissible Reference Frames imply different causal structures / terminal economic model classes. When multiple narratives collapse into one owner-return equation, ordinary variable underwriting is cleaner.**

This is a successful negative result.

GigaDevice and Midea now point in different directions.


```text
growth narrative
-> different source of reinvestment
but
-> same retention x incremental-ROIC translation
-> same payout / share-count / multiple bridge
```

Analysis Divergence adds limited incremental information beyond good variable underwriting.

### Method hypothesis after two species

> **Use multi-frame divergence selectively when Reference Frame uncertainty changes the causal model class. Do not invoke it merely because a company has several strategic narratives.**

This is stronger and narrower than creating an `Analysis Divergence framework` for every Research case.

It also supports the emerging economic-species idea in a restrained form:

> choose an underwriting language appropriate to the economic species before deciding whether a Frame battle is even useful.

No Economic Species schema or Constitution change follows from two dogfoods.
